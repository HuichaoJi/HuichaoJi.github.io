'Tight-Fitting' vs. 'Loose-Fitting' as a Visual Primitive for Event Perception



 Here are some demonstrations of the various conditions discussed in the following paper:
Ji, H., & Scholl, B. J. (XXX).'Tight-fitting' vs. 'Loose-fitting' as a visual primitive for event perception.
These demonstrations are provided as MP4 files, which can be downloaded or viewed directly in most web-browsers. These movies are a bit large and choppy, but they should be sufficient to illustrate the basic conditions. As highly compressed versions of the original stimuli, these movies may not preserve the precise spatial and temporal characteristics of the originals.  
 
The stability of interacting objects (e.g. whether your ring stays on your finger) often depends not on spatial relations (e.g. 'in'/'on'), but on whether they fit tightly or loosely. This distinction is so important that it's lexically prioritized in some languages (e.g. Korean). Given its functional significance, might it be prioritized even in visual processing, and even in monolingual English speakers? Across six experiments (N=380), observers viewed sequential pairs of animations (e.g. objects moving into containers) and simply detected whether they were identical or different. Observers were better at detecting Across-Fit changes (tight vs. loose) than Within-Fit changes (e.g. between different tight-fit animations), even when task-irrelevant - and when the latter involved greater objective differences. 'Tight'/'loose' was even prioritized over 'in/on', in a way that couldn't be explained by lower-level properties. This work shows how a new form of intuitive physics may serve as a visual primitive for event perception.
